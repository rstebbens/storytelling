# Voice

Voice is the part of the writing that belongs to the author.

Editing can improve clarity, structure, pacing and precision.

It should not make the author sound like somebody else.

The aim is not to produce the most polished version of the prose.

The aim is to produce the clearest version of the author's voice.

---

# Write Like The Author On Their Best Day

Preserve the way the author naturally thinks and speaks.

Improve the writing without replacing the person writing it.

Ask:

* Would this person actually say this?
* Does the vocabulary belong to them?
* Does the rhythm sound natural when read aloud?
* Have I improved something because it was unclear, or simply because another version sounds more polished?
* Has editing made the writing more recognisable or less?

The strongest edit often sounds like the author had a particularly good writing day.

It should not sound like an editor took over.

---

# Learn The Voice From Evidence

Do not invent an author's voice from a short description of their personality.

Where previous writing is available, study it.

Look for recurring characteristics such as:

* sentence length and rhythm
* vocabulary
* humour
* directness
* use of fragments
* understatement
* exaggeration
* digressions
* rhetorical questions
* conversational asides
* how the author handles emotion
* how they move between serious and funny moments

Patterns across several pieces of writing are stronger evidence than any single sentence.

Voice should be discovered from the author's writing, not imposed upon it.

---

# Preserve Imperfection

Human voices are rarely grammatically perfect.

They contain fragments.

Unexpected turns of phrase.

Occasionally strange word choices.

Sentences that are longer or shorter than an editor might prefer.

These are not automatically defects.

Correct mistakes that interfere with meaning.

Keep imperfections that contribute to personality.

Before changing an unusual phrase, ask whether it is unclear or merely unusual.

Those are different problems.

---

# Protect Humour

Humour is particularly vulnerable to editing.

A joke may depend on:

* timing
* brevity
* an unexpected word
* understatement
* exaggeration
* a pause
* an awkward observation
* self-deprecation
* something deliberately left unexplained

Do not improve a joke by explaining it.

Do not replace understated humour with a more obvious punchline.

Do not make the author sound wittier than they naturally are.

If the humour already works, leave it alone.

---

# Keep Conversational Rhythm

Good conversational writing does not have a perfectly regular rhythm.

Some thoughts arrive in complete sentences.

Others don't.

Paragraphs may occasionally contain a single sentence because that is where the thought naturally lands.

Read the writing aloud.

If it sounds written rather than spoken, investigate why.

But do not manufacture conversational style by filling the prose with fragments, ellipses, rhetorical questions or one-line paragraphs.

Natural conversation varies.

So should the writing.

---

# Don't Upgrade The Vocabulary

Prefer the words the author would naturally choose.

Do not replace ordinary language with more impressive alternatives simply because they sound literary, professional or sophisticated.

Be particularly suspicious of words that make the author sound:

* more academic
* more corporate
* more sentimental
* more profound
* more certain

than the source material suggests.

Clarity is an improvement.

Vocabulary inflation usually isn't.

---

# Preserve Emotional Restraint

Do not increase the emotional temperature of a story simply because the subject allows it.

If the author handles something difficult with humour, understatement or matter-of-fact observation, respect that choice.

Do not add emotional language to tell the reader how important, painful, joyful or meaningful something was.

Let events and observations carry emotional weight.

Sometimes restraint is the voice.

---

# Preserve Contradiction

People are inconsistent.

They can be confident and uncertain.

Generous and irritated.

Serious and ridiculous.

Certain they are right and later discover they weren't.

Do not edit these contradictions away simply to make the narrator appear more coherent.

Contradiction often makes a narrator believable.

It may also be where the story lives.

---

# Avoid Generic Author Voice

AI and heavy editing tend to pull different writers towards the same centre.

Warning signs include:

* polished inspirational language
* neat rhetorical contrasts
* perfectly balanced sentences
* excessive punchy fragments
* repeated three-part lists
* predictable rhetorical questions
* quotation-ready lessons
* unnecessary emotional emphasis
* corporate vocabulary appearing in personal writing
* every paragraph sounding deliberately crafted

If the same passage could plausibly have been written by almost anyone, some of the author's voice may have been lost.

---

# Don't Confuse Voice With Habit

Not everything an author repeatedly does needs protecting.

Writers have habits as well as voices.

Repetition, filler, unclear sentences and favourite constructions may still need editing.

The test is whether changing them makes the writing:

* clearer
* more precise
* easier to follow

without making it less recognisable.

Protect personality.

Do not protect clutter.

---

# Match Voice To Context

The same person does not sound identical everywhere.

A personal story, LinkedIn post, speech, email and executive update may require different levels of formality.

The underlying voice should remain recognisable while adapting to the audience and purpose.

Do not confuse consistency with sameness.

---

# The Read-Aloud Test

Read the finished piece aloud.

Then ask:

> Does this sound like something this person would actually say?

If the answer is no, find out why.

The problem may be vocabulary.

It may be rhythm.

It may be excessive polish.

It may be a sentence designed to impress rather than communicate.

Fix that before changing anything else.

---

# Final Principle

Editing should remove the things that get between the author and the reader.

It should not remove the author.

The finished writing should feel clearer, stronger and more deliberate while remaining unmistakably theirs.
